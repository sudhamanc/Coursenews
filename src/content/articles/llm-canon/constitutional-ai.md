Somewhere in the machinery of a safe chatbot sits a stack of transcripts no one
wants to read: the outputs a model produces when deliberately provoked toward
cruelty, deception, and worse. InstructGPT's approach to harmlessness required
human beings to read exactly that material and rate it, in volume, so a reward
model could learn what to avoid. It is unpleasant work, it does not scale, and it
leaves the model's values buried illegibly in a matrix of weights. In late 2022,
Anthropic proposed a different arrangement in a paper titled *Constitutional AI:
Harmlessness from AI Feedback*. Write the principles down. Let the model apply
them to itself. Then train it on its own judgments.

## The Unscalable Cost of Saying No

In the running argument of Part IV — each paper deleting a moving part from the
stack before it — Constitutional AI removes the human labelers, but only from the
harmlessness half of the pipeline. It keeps InstructGPT's two-stage shape
entirely: a supervised phase followed by a reinforcement-learning phase against a
learned preference. What changes is where the supervision comes from. This is a
form of **scalable oversight**: rather than hiring people to annotate an
ever-growing pile of harmful examples, the method asks whether a capable model
can supervise itself against a fixed set of rules. One detail is routinely
misreported and worth stating plainly — the human labels for *helpfulness* are
retained. Only harmlessness is handed to the machine.

## A Set of Principles You Can Read

The rules live in a **constitution**: a compact list of natural-language
principles — drawn from sources as varied as human-rights declarations — that
describe what a good response should and should not do. This is the paper's
quiet governance contribution. In ordinary RLHF, a model's normative commitments
are an emergent property of which examples annotators happened to see; you cannot
inspect them, audit them, or amend them. A constitution turns those commitments
into a document you can edit with a text editor.

## Phase One — The Model Corrects Itself

The first phase is a supervised **critique-and-revision** loop. A helpful-only
model is fed red-team prompts and, predictably, produces a harmful response. The
same model is then asked to *critique* what it just wrote, judged against a
principle sampled from the constitution — to name, in its own words, how the
response violated the rule. Finally it is asked to *revise* the response in light
of that critique. The loop runs several times, drawing different principles on
each pass, and the model is fine-tuned on the final, revised answers. The result
is a model the paper calls SL-CAI, which already declines most harmful requests
without ever having seen a human harmlessness label.

## Phase Two — Learning From Its Own Preferences

The second phase is **RLAIF** — reinforcement learning from AI feedback, the
mirror image of InstructGPT's reward-model stage with the human replaced. SL-CAI
samples pairs of responses to harmful prompts. Each pair is handed to a feedback
model alongside a constitutional principle, framed as a multiple-choice question:
which response better satisfies this rule? The model's log-probabilities over the
options become a soft preference label. A preference model is trained on these
machine-made labels, and the policy is optimized against it with reinforcement
learning. The authors found that letting the feedback model reason step by step
before choosing — chain-of-thought in the judging step — measurably improved the
quality of its labels.

## Helpful About Being Harmless

The headline result was not merely that the constitutional models were more
harmless — it was that they were *less evasive* while being so. A blunt RLHF model
tends to meet an uncomfortable prompt with a flat refusal; the constitutional
models instead explained their objection, engaging with the request enough to say
why they would not comply. This **non-evasive refusal** is the practically
important gain, because a wall of "I can't help with that" is both unhelpful and
easy to route around. Crucially, the harmlessness-versus-helpfulness trade-off did
not tighten; the Pareto frontier moved outward, with the model getting safer
without getting duller.

## Why It Matters

Constitutional AI underpins Claude, but its wider influence is the shift it
kicked off across the industry toward AI-generated preference data. Once a model
could produce usable preference labels, the human annotator stopped being the
rate-limiting reagent of alignment — a change that feeds directly into the
data-generation side of the DPO-style pipelines and the reward design of GRPO that
follow in this section. And by making a model's values a legible document rather
than a statistical residue of who happened to label what, it reframed alignment as
something you can govern, not just train.

The legitimacy question does not vanish; it moves. The principles are still chosen
by a single lab, so "aligned to whom" becomes "whose constitution." The scheme
also leans on a capable base model — a system too weak to understand its own
principles cannot critique itself against them — and the robustness of the
self-critique step to adversarial pressure remains open. What Anthropic
demonstrated is narrower and more durable than a solution: that a large fraction
of the human labor in alignment was a component, and that it too could be removed.
