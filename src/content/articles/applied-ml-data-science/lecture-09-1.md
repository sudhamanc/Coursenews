K-means is fast, simple, and quietly opinionated. Underneath its arithmetic sits an
assumption that clusters are convex, roughly spherical, and about the same size —
that every direction matters equally. Real data rarely obliges. Points curl into
crescents, wander along filaments, nest inside one another, and blur at their edges.
This lecture assembles three clustering methods that each relax a different one of
k-means' assumptions: density-based clustering that follows arbitrary shapes,
hierarchical clustering that builds a tree instead of a flat partition, and
probabilistic mixtures that admit a point can partly belong to several groups at once.

## Density Finds the Shape

**DBSCAN** — Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise — abandons
centroids entirely and reasons about crowding. It takes two hyperparameters: `eps`,
the radius of a neighborhood, and `min_samples`, the number of points that must fall
inside that radius for a region to count as dense. From these it sorts every point
into one of three roles. A **core point** has at least `min_samples` neighbors within
`eps`; a **border point** sits inside a core point's neighborhood but is not itself
dense; and anything left over is a **noise point**. Clusters grow by chaining
together core points that are within reach of one another and sweeping in their
borders.

```python
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
dbscan = DBSCAN(eps=0.05, min_samples=5)
dbscan.fit(X)
np.unique(dbscan.labels_)   # e.g. array([-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6])
```

The label $-1$ marks noise. Because DBSCAN never assumes a shape, it happily
recovers a spiral or a ring that would defeat k-means, and it identifies outliers as
a matter of course. One quirk: it has no `predict()` method, since it defines clusters
only over the points it has seen. To classify new points, practitioners pair it with a
classifier — training a k-nearest-neighbors model on the core samples and their labels.
Its density instinct has since been refined into **HDBSCAN**, a hierarchical variant
that copes with clusters of differing densities.

## Building Clusters Bottom-Up

**Agglomerative clustering** takes the opposite view of structure. It begins with
every point as its own singleton cluster, then repeatedly merges the two most similar
clusters, and repeats until a stopping criterion — usually a target number of clusters —
is reached. The record of those merges forms a **dendrogram**, a tree that shows not
just a single partition but the entire nested hierarchy, letting you read off two,
three, or four clusters by cutting the tree at different heights.

What "most similar" means is set by the **linkage** criterion. `ward`, the default,
merges the pair that produces the least increase in total within-cluster variance and
tends to yield evenly sized clusters. `average` uses the mean distance between all
cross-cluster pairs, `complete` uses the maximum (farthest) distance, and `single`
uses the minimum (nearest) distance — the last prone to chaining long, straggly
clusters together.

```python
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
agg = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=5, linkage='ward')
agg.fit(X)
```

Evaluating any of this without labels is genuinely hard. When ground-truth labels
exist they settle the question; when they do not, several partitions may be equally
defensible, and the honest recourse is *task-based evaluation* — judging a clustering
by how much it helps a downstream job, such as whether cluster-derived features improve
a supervised model.

## Soft Membership: Gaussian Mixtures

The final method treats clustering as a question of probability. A **Gaussian Mixture
Model** assumes the data was generated by drawing from one of $K$ Gaussian components,
each with its own mean $\mu_k$, covariance $\Sigma_k$, and mixing weight $\pi_k$. The
density of any point is the weighted sum

$$
p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k),
$$

and fitting the model means choosing the parameters that maximize the likelihood of
the whole dataset, $p(X) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$.
Unlike k-means, a mixture gives each point a *soft* assignment: a set of probabilities
of belonging to each component rather than a single verdict.

### The EM Loop

That likelihood cannot be maximized in one shot, because we do not know which
component produced each point. **Expectation-Maximization** breaks the deadlock by
alternating two steps. The E-step computes each point's **responsibility** — the
posterior probability that component $k$ generated point $i$:

$$
\gamma(z_{ik}) = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}.
$$

The M-step then re-estimates the parameters as responsibility-weighted averages, with
$N_k = \sum_{i} \gamma(z_{ik})$:

$$
\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i} \gamma(z_{ik})\, x_i, \qquad
\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{m},
$$

and $\Sigma_k$ as the weighted covariance about the new mean. The two steps repeat
until the parameters — or the likelihood — converge.

```python
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, n_init=10)
gm.fit(X)
gm.predict_proba(X[:1])   # soft membership over the 3 components
```

A mixture's covariance type controls its flexibility: `full` lets each component take
any ellipsoidal shape, `spherical` forces round components of possibly different
sizes, `diag` allows axis-aligned ellipsoids, and `tied` shares one shape across all.
Because it estimates density, a fitted GMM also does **anomaly detection** — points in
low-density regions, below some percentile of `score_samples`, are flagged as unusual.

## Picking the Number of Components

How many components should a mixture have? Raw likelihood always improves with more
components, so it cannot decide alone. Two penalized criteria do the balancing:

$$
\text{BIC} = p \log m - 2 \log \hat{L}, \qquad \text{AIC} = 2p - 2 \log \hat{L},
$$

where $m$ is the number of instances, $p$ the number of parameters, and $\hat{L}$ the
maximized likelihood. Both reward fit and punish complexity; the model with the lowest
score wins. A **Bayesian Gaussian Mixture** goes further, using a weight-concentration
prior that can switch unnecessary components off automatically, letting the data
suggest its own cluster count.

## Why It Matters

The four algorithms form a toolkit rather than a ranking. K-means is the fast default;
agglomerative clustering reveals hierarchy through its dendrogram; DBSCAN discovers
arbitrarily shaped clusters and isolates outliers; and Gaussian mixtures generalize
k-means with genuine covariance and calibrated, probabilistic memberships. Knowing which
assumption each one relaxes is what separates clustering that merely runs from clustering
that reflects the real geometry of the data — the difference between forcing round holes
on square pegs and letting the data keep its own shape.
